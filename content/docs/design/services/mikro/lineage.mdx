## The folder full of `_decon_crop_seg_final2.tif`

You know this folder. A raw stack, a cropped version of it, a deconvolved version of the crop, a
mask, a spreadsheet of measurements and a maximum projection made for the figure. The only thing
connecting them is the file name, and the only thing saying where the crop sat in the original
stack is your memory.

This breaks in three well-known ways:

- **The link to the raw data is lost.** Six months later, nobody can say for certain which stack a
  mask was made from, or whether it was made before or after the deconvolution.
- **The calibration is lost, or copied.** Either the result has no pixel size at all, or it carries
  its own copy, which stays wrong when the original is corrected.
- **Overlaying means re-aligning.** To see the mask on the raw image, or the crop in the overview,
  someone has to line them up again by hand.

Mikro treats all three as one problem. When an analysis writes a result, it also writes **one edge
back to the data it was computed from**. That edge is the *lineage*, and it is also what puts the
result in the right place.

## One edge, two jobs

The [coordinate systems](/docs/design/services/mikro/coordinate-systems) page explains that every
piece of data in Mikro lives in a space of its own, and that spaces are connected by
transformations. A derived dataset follows the same rule. It does **not** move into its parent's
space. It gets its own pixel grid, and an edge from that grid back to the source:

<MikroLineage />

Follow the arrows from the mask, and you have its history: it came from the deconvolved crop, which
came from the crop, which came from the tile.

Read as a route, it is the mask's place in the world: follow the arrows and you arrive on the
slide, in millimetres. Nobody registered the mask. It reaches the slide through the edges of the
data it was made from, and the pixel size and stage position still exist exactly once, on the tile.

That is the whole idea: **a result ends up where its source is, because the record of where it
came from is the map.**

## A day at the microscope, step by step

**You crop the interesting region out of the tile.** The crop is a new dataset. Its edge back to
the tile says "same pixels, shifted by this much". Open the crop in the overview scene and it sits
on the spot it was cut from. Because a crop only cuts and does not compute, your contrast settings
and histogram from the tile are still valid for it.

**You deconvolve the crop.** Same grid, so the edge is an identity: no pixel moved. The values did
change, though. They are still intensities, but they are new numbers, so the contrast limits you
set on the raw crop no longer apply.

**You segment it.** Again no pixel moved, so again an identity. But the values now mean something
else entirely: pixel value 7 is not "brightness 7", it is "cell number 7". Mikro records this, so
when it sets up a scene for the mask it shows a label map, instead of a very dark grayscale
picture.

**You measure every cell.** The result is a table with one row per cell. It was clearly made from
the mask, so it has an edge back to it. But a row is a cell, and a cell is not a point: asking
"where is row 7" has no answer. Mikro records the lineage and states honestly that there is no
geometry to go with it.

**You make a maximum projection for the figure.** The projection keeps `y` and `x` and drops `z`.
Its edge says exactly that: these two axes correspond, the third one is gone. It still lines up
with the stack from above, and it makes no claim about depth.

**You fuse two channels acquired on different cameras.** A result can have several sources. They
are recorded in order, and the first one is the one that places the result.

## Space and values are two separate questions

Each derivation edge answers two questions independently. Keeping them apart is what lets a viewer
do the right thing without being told.

<MikroSpaceValues />

| What you did | What happened to space | What happened to the values | What you notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crop | shifted | identical | sits where it was cut from, contrast settings still fit |
| Downsample | rescaled | (as stated by the tool) | overlays at the right size |
| Deconvolve, denoise, normalize | nothing | transformed | overlays exactly, needs its own contrast |
| Threshold, segment | nothing | categorized | overlays exactly, staged as a label map |
| Project, line profile | some axes dropped | (as stated by the tool) | lines up along the axes that remain |
| Measure per object | no correspondence | transformed | appears as a table, linked to the mask, not drawn anywhere |

The three answers to the values question are worth remembering:

- **identical**: the numbers are the same numbers. Anything computed on the source's values,
  histograms and contrast limits included, still holds.
- **transformed**: the same kind of quantity with new numbers. Still an intensity, but nothing you
  tuned on the source carries across.
- **categorized**: the values became labels. Colormaps and gamma are meaningless on them.

## When there is no "where"

Not every result has a position, and Mikro does not pretend otherwise. A measurement table is
related to its mask, but no point of one maps to a point of the other. Recording that as "same
place" would be a lie, and recording nothing would lose the lineage. So there is a third option: an
edge that says *related, with no point correspondence*.

This matters when you try to put something into a scene and it does not appear. Mikro tells you
which of two very different situations you are in:

- **Not registered yet.** Nothing relates this data to the scene so far. This is a gap, and
  somebody can close it by registering the data.
- **Cannot be placed.** The data lost its geometry in the step that produced it. There is no
  missing registration to look for. This is a fact about the result, not a mistake.

A table that cannot be drawn is still fully usable. Hover a cell in the mask and its row shows up,
because the mask's pixel values are keyed to the table's rows. This lookup even works when you
hover the *raw image*: Mikro follows the derivation from the image to the mask that was made from
it, and from there into the table. See
[attribute plans](/docs/design/services/mikro/coordinate-systems#attribute-plans-what-is-under-my-cursor)
for how that works.

## Derived is a fact, registered is a claim

There are two reasons two datasets can line up, and they are not equally certain.

A **derivation** is written by the step that made the data. The crop tool knows where it cut. That
knowledge is a fact about the data, it is the same in every scene, and it travels with the dataset.
It is exactly as reliable as the tool that wrote it.

A **registration** is someone's statement about how two independent things relate: this tile sits
here on the slide, this electron micrograph matches that fluorescence image. It may be read from
metadata, drawn by hand or computed by an algorithm, and it can be refined later.

Mikro keeps the two apart and shows you how much to trust the combination. Every placement in a
scene reports:

- **how well it is known**: guessed, read from acquisition metadata, authored by someone, or
  checked against the data. A chain is only as good as its weakest link, so one guessed
  registration marks everything that depends on it.
- **what survives the trip**: whether a distance measured in this view is a real distance. Through
  a plain shift or rotation it is. Through a uniform rescale, shapes and angles hold and lengths
  need one factor. Through a general affine registration, angles and distances no longer mean what
  they appear to.

<MikroPlacementTrust />

When a registration is refined, every result derived from that image moves with it. None of them
had a copy to go stale.

## Questions you can now ask

- **Where did this come from?** Every dataset lists the data it was computed from. Following
  those steps one by one leads back to an acquisition.
- **What was made from this?** Every dataset lists what was computed from it: other images, and
  also the tables, meshes and annotations.
- **Show me only what the microscope produced.** Datasets can be filtered to those that were
  acquired rather than computed.
- **Is this overlay trustworthy?** The placement says how it is known, and through which steps.
- **Are my micrometres still micrometres here?** The placement says which measurements survive.

## What lineage is not

Three neighbouring ideas are recorded elsewhere, on purpose:

- **Which file it was imported from.** A dataset converted from a `.czi` remembers the file and the
  series inside it. That says which *bytes* were read. It relates to no space, because a file has
  none. A dataset can have both a source file and a source dataset.
- **Who ran it, and why.** The edge says *what* a result was computed from. Which person, app and
  task produced it is recorded in the
  [provenance audit trail](/docs/design/architecture/provenance), for every change.
- **What it means biologically.** That cell 7 is a neuron of a given type in a given animal belongs
  in the knowledge graph, [Kraph](/docs/design/services/kraph).

<Callout type="info" title="One thing to remember">

A result does not need to be told where it is. It needs to say what it was made from, and how.
Everything else, its pixel size, its place on the slide, its overlay on the raw image, follows from
the data it came from.

</Callout>

## For the people writing the analysis

Lineage is recorded by the tool that creates the result, in one argument. If you write analysis
apps, this is the part that is your job:

```python
# same grid, new numbers: a deconvolution, a denoise
derived_from=[volume.derive_identity(value_relation="TRANSFORMED")]

# same grid, values became labels: a segmentation
derived_from=[volume.derive_identity(value_relation="CATEGORIZED")]

# a crop: the result's first voxel lands at `origin` in the source
derived_from=[volume.derive_translation(origin)]

# a projection that keeps y and x
derived_from=[volume.derive_projection(["y", "x"])]
```

An identity edge is a promise that the result really is on the source's grid, and the server does
not re-check it, so compare shapes and axis order before you make it.

A per-object measurement table needs two things. Its lineage names the mask and leaves the
transform out, which is what records "related, no geometry". Separately, its id column says which
mask's pixel values it is identified by. Only that second statement makes the rows show up on
hover:

```python
mikro.create_table_dataset(
    name="cell measurements",
    data=df,
    derived_from=[
        DatasetDerivedFromInput(dataset=mask.id, value_relation="TRANSFORMED")
    ],
    columns=[
        ColumnInput(
            name="instance",
            axis_type=AxisType.INDEX,
            identified_by=[DatasetIdentifiesInput(kind="DATASET", dataset=mask.id)],
        )
    ],
)
```

The [Python guide on reading and writing data](/docs/developers/python/types)
shows a complete action.
