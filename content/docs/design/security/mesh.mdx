Reaching your platform from the same local network is easy. Reaching it from *the
train*, *a collaborator's institute*, or *a GPU node behind a different NAT* is
where most systems force an ugly trade-off: expose ports to the internet, or route
everything through a central server that then sees all your data. The **mesh** is
designed to avoid both.

The mesh is best understood as a **personal, private mesh** in the spirit of
Tailscale, not a federation of public servers. It lets your authenticated
endpoints, your laptop, your lab workstation, a remote compute node, find and
reach each other from anywhere, without poking holes in your firewall.

## Control plane vs. data plane

The "coordinator" here is the same **coordinator** role described in [Identity &
Access](/docs/design/security/identity), the control plane that handles auth and
discovery, now doing its discovery job across networks. It is not a separate,
mysterious box: it is the coordination side of the platform, run as a dedicated
*coordination server* so that endpoints on different networks can still meet. (The
community-operated coordination server is a shared instance of that same role.)

The security of the mesh rests on one clean separation:

- **Control plane (the coordinator).** A small central service keeps track of who
  you are and which platforms and apps belong to you, and helps two endpoints find
  each other and punch through NAT. It handles **authentication, discovery, and
  signaling only**. It knows *that* two endpoints want to talk.
- **Data plane (the direct connection).** Once the introduction is made, the actual
  workload traffic, your images, your tables, your task arguments and results,
  flows **directly peer-to-peer** between endpoints over an encrypted **WireGuard**
  tunnel. It never round-trips through the coordinator.

<Callout type="info" title="The coordinator never sees your data">
This is the single most important privacy property of the mesh: the coordinator
handles **auth and discovery only**, and your **workload traffic flows directly
peer-to-peer and never passes through the central server**. The mesh is
*centrally-coordinated*, but it is *not* centralized in the way that matters for
privacy. The operator of the coordinator cannot see your images, your results, or
any of the data moving between your nodes.
</Callout>

## Why this is the secure default

- **Nothing is exposed to the open internet.** Endpoints reach each other through
  authenticated mesh connections, so you don't publish your platform on a public IP
  or open inbound firewall ports.
- **Traffic is encrypted end to end.** The data plane is a WireGuard tunnel between
  your own machines; even the relay fallback (below) carries already-encrypted
  traffic.
- **Access follows identity.** Joining the mesh authenticates the endpoint against
  the coordinator, so only *your* authenticated machines can discover and reach one
  another. This is the same identity model described in [Identity &
  Access](/docs/design/security/identity).

Under the hood the coordinator is built on
[**Ionscale**](https://github.com/jsiebens/ionscale), a self-hostable Tailscale
control plane, so you can run the control plane yourself rather than trusting a
third party with it.

## Relay fallback

In the great majority of cases two endpoints establish a direct peer-to-peer
connection, even across NAT. Occasionally a particularly restrictive network
refuses to cooperate and no direct path can be formed. When that happens the mesh
falls back to **relaying** the traffic so the connection still works, at some cost
to throughput. Importantly, the relayed traffic is still the same encrypted
WireGuard traffic, the relay moves bytes it cannot read. This fallback is the
exception, and the mesh always prefers a direct connection first.

## The mesh is optional: bring your own connectivity

The mesh is a convenience, not a requirement. Arkitekt is just a set of services
speaking over the network, so **any** way of connecting your endpoints works
equally well. You are never locked into ours. In particular, you can:

- **Bring your own Tailscale client.** If your group already runs Tailscale (or
  another WireGuard-based network), point your endpoints at that instead. The mesh
  is Tailscale-flavoured precisely so this feels familiar, but there is nothing
  stopping you from using your existing tailnet.
- **Use your institution's VPN.** If your university or institute already provides a
  VPN that puts your machines on the same virtual network, that is often all you
  need to reach your platform, no mesh involved. See
  [Using a VPN](/docs/design/various/vpn).
- **Turn it off entirely.** On a single trusted LAN you may not need any of this.
  You can simply **not join the mesh** (or leave it) and reach your platform over
  your local network like any other service.

The point is that the security boundary lives in Arkitekt's
[authentication and authorization](/docs/design/security/identity), not in the
transport. However you choose to move the bytes between your machines, the platform
still authenticates every request.

<Callout type="warn" title="The mesh is optional and still young">
The mesh is one of the newer, **optional and experimental** parts of the platform
and is evolving quickly. It is now the only built-in way to discover a deployment
across networks (local-network advertising has been removed). For the commands to
actually join a platform to the mesh (`arkitekt mesh join` / `arkitekt mesh leave`),
see [the mesh](/docs/introduction/installation#arkitekt-mesh) in the installation
guide.
</Callout>
